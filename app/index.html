How the tiebreaker is scored

If teams tie on (a), the six probabilities from (b) and (c) decide. They are scored with the Brier score: the average of (probability − outcome)², where the outcome is 1 if the Democrat wins and 0 if not. Lower is better.

The Brier score rewards honest probabilities. Pushing a number toward 0 or 1 only pays off if the race really is that certain, so we enter the model's probabilities as they are.

What do the intervals mean?

Each simulation ends with a seat count. The 80% interval holds the middle 80% of those counts: in 8 of 10 simulated elections, Democrats end up inside it. The 95% interval holds the middle 95%. The median is the count in the middle, with half the simulations above and half below.

The Senate counts include the 34 Democratic and independent seats that are not on the ballot this year. Democrats need 51 seats because Vice President Vance breaks a 50–50 tie for Republicans.

What do safe, likely, lean and toss-up mean?

The labels translate our chance of winning into words, the same for both parties:

Toss-up · 45% to 55% Lean · 55% to 65% Likely · 65% to 95% Safe · 95% or more

These bands are narrower than most forecasters use. Split Ticket, for example, calls a race a toss-up until one side reaches 60%, lean up to 80% and likely up to 95%. Even so, the favourite in a Lean race still loses 35% to 45% of the time.

The method step by step
  1. Polls. Newer polls, larger samples and likely-voter samples count more. Polls paid for by a party or campaign count half. A pollster's older polls in the same race count less. Polls of hypothetical matchups are dropped. Polls VoteHub lacks are added by hand after the news check.
  2. Prediction markets. Prices from both venues are averaged by trading volume. A market counts like a poll average: $20,000 traded is worth one poll, $1 million or more is worth ten.
  3. Fundamentals. For the House we use Decision Desk HQ's district probabilities of 22 October, the course's starting point. For the Senate: the 2024 presidential result in the state compared with the nation, plus the national environment of , plus 3 points for an incumbent.
  4. Combining. Each input becomes an expected margin with its own uncertainty, averaged with weights inversely proportional to their variance. No race is treated as more certain than ±4 points, because the inputs overlap.
  5. Simulation. In each of 20,000 simulated elections every race moves with a shared national error of ±3.6 points (fat-tailed), plus regional, state and race-specific errors. Wins by independents (Nebraska's Osborn, Alaska's Hill) are not counted as Democratic seats.
  6. What we leave out. Other forecasters' models, expert ratings and crowd forecasts (Metaculus, Manifold, PredictIt) are not inputs. We show them for comparison.
